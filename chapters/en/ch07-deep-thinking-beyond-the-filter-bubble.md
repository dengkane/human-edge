# 07. Deep Thinking

> **The one thing to take away:** you do not need an algorithm to keep you inside your own view. You
> do that yourself — which is why the fix is structural, not a matter of trying harder.

It is late, and you are reading about something you already have an opinion on. Every link you tap,
every comment you open, confirms what you walked in believing. Nobody has trapped you. You are picking
them — the satisfying ones — and scrolling past everything else, and it feels like being informed.

The popular version of this chapter blames the feed. Someone, somewhere, arranged the walls.

The evidence says the walls are yours. And that changes the fix entirely.

## Why this matters now

Chapter 06 was a deadline: deciding when you could not prove the choice. This chapter is the other half
of the pair, and it comes *before* the deadline. It is the clarity you bring into a decision rather
than the nerve you find at the end of one.

The popular story about why clear thinking is hard goes like this: algorithms watch what you click,
they feed you more of the same, and you end up sealed inside a *filter bubble* — a personalised world
where your existing views are all you see, and you never notice the walls.

That story is compelling, it is everywhere, and the evidence for it is much weaker than you have been
told. Which matters here not because I want to argue about social media, but because **the story is
load-bearing for how you would fix the problem — and it points at the wrong fix.**

## The bubble you were told about

Start with the strongest available summary of the actual research. A literature review by the Reuters
Institute for the Study of Journalism, supported by the Royal Society, looked at the evidence on echo
chambers, filter bubbles and polarisation. Its own summary of the findings:

> the work reviewed here suggests echo chambers are **much less widespread than is commonly assumed**,
> [and] finds no support for the filter bubble.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/echo-chambers-filter-bubbles-and-polarisation-literature-review -->

The same review notes that these terms are used in public debate "not in ways that are always aligned
with, or based on, scientific work."

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/echo-chambers-filter-bubbles-and-polarisation-literature-review -->

Now the part that has to be handled honestly, because the naive reading of that finding is wrong too.
Echo chambers are not imaginary. A large study of Facebook and Twitter found that **homophilic
clustering — people grouping with the like-minded — dominates online interaction** on both platforms.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2023301118 -->

So both of these are true at once: people **do** sort into like-minded groups, and the mechanism usually
blamed for it — an algorithm quietly enclosing an individual — is not what the data support.

The reconciliation is where this chapter starts, and it is a distinction that changes what you do about
it. Homophilic clustering shows up in **interaction** — who engages with whom — and that is the layer
Cinelli and colleagues measured. What that does not establish is that the information supply is
neutral; the same paper treats *bias in information diffusion* as a second ingredient, and I have
flattened that into a cleaner contrast than the evidence supports. Treat the exposure/engagement split
as the shape of the problem, not as a measured decomposition.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2023301118 -->

What does survive, and is the point here: **the pull toward agreement is not something done to you.**
You are choosing a smaller world out of a larger one — and you are doing it on purpose, because the
smaller one feels better.

This matters because it takes away the fix everyone reaches for. If the walls were put up around you,
you could knock them down from outside: diversify your feed, follow people you disagree with, delete
the app. If the walls are built by your own attention, changing the supply does nothing — because the
supply was never the constraint.

## The remedy that does not work

So the obvious fix is: be more open-minded. Deliberately seek opposing views. Hold your conclusions
loosely. Postpone judgment until you have considered alternatives.

These are good instincts, and there is real research behind them. Psychologists measure this cluster of
dispositions and call it **actively open-minded thinking** — willingness to consider alternative
opinions, sensitivity to evidence that contradicts your current beliefs, willingness to postpone
closure, and reflective thought.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9966223/ -->

And it works — for most things. People who score high on these measures are better at
heuristics-and-biases tasks. They are less prone to superstitious thinking, and less likely to believe
conspiracy theories.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9966223/ -->

Now the finding this chapter is actually built on. The same researchers report a paradox, in their own
words:

> although AOT scales are potent predictors of performance on most rational thinking tasks, **they do
> not predict the avoidance of myside thinking, even though it is virtually the quintessence of the AOT
> concept.**

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9966223/ -->

Read that carefully. At first it looks like a technicality. It is actually devastating.

**Myside bias** is the tendency to evaluate evidence by whose side it favours. It is what the filter
bubble metaphor is groping toward — the reason your views survive contact with contradictory
information. It is the thing open-mindedness is *for*.

And the disposition named after open-mindedness does not predict whether you avoid it.

So the advice "be more open-minded about your own beliefs" is not a weaker version of the fix. It is
**documented not to work on the specific failure this chapter is about.** Being the kind of person who
values considering alternatives tells you almost nothing about whether you will apply that to the
belief you are defending right now.

That should sound familiar. Every chapter in this book has the same shape. The remedy everyone
prescribes is the one the evidence declines to support, and what is left is more specific and less
comfortable.

### Why effort is not the answer

The reason is not mysterious, and it explains why "try harder" fails even when you do try harder.

Confirmation bias is not a failure of attention. It is what happens when you are **motivated to keep a
belief**. The classic review describes people treating evidence in a biased way specifically "when they
are motivated by the desire to defend beliefs that they wish to maintain."

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/nickersonConfirmationBias.pdf -->

Look at where the motivation sits in that sentence. It is attached to *the belief*, not to the
information. Which means the bias will survive any amount of information you encounter, because the
information was never the input being protected.

Later work tested the obvious intervention directly — literally instructing people to "put on their
thinking cap" before evaluating scientific evidence. The finding is that this does not remove the bias;
the fullest accounts of motivated cognition keep showing people evaluating evidence consistent with
their priors.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://moodle2.units.it/pluginfile.php/433142/mod_resource/content/1/Drummond%20%20Fischhoff%20%282019%29.pdf -->

An instruction to be careful is a disposition-level intervention. Myside bias lives a level below that.

So the fix has to be **structural** — something outside your intention that changes what happens
regardless of how motivated you are to reach a particular conclusion. Three structures, each one
attackable, each one checkable.

## Structure one: the adversarial pass

Instead of trying to be fair, **assign a role.**

Write your actual position, in one or two sentences, as a claim someone could disagree with — the same
move Chapter 05 used to surface a frame. Then, in writing, argue the other side as if you were being
paid to win. Not "some might say", which is a way of holding a position at arm's length while keeping
it. A real case, at full strength.

The difference from "be open-minded" is that the role removes the motivation. You are not trying to
abandon your view; you are performing an opponent. The performance has an output — an argument you can
read afterwards — which means it can be evaluated. A disposition cannot.

This is also the single best use of the machine in this chapter, and it is worth being precise about
why. A language model will argue the opposite of whatever you ask it to, competently and at length,
with no stake in the outcome. That is exactly the adversarial pass, generated on demand — and its lack
of a stake, which is a defect everywhere else in this book, is precisely what makes it useful here. It
is not trying to protect anything.

Two cautions, both learned from Chapter 02. Ask it to argue the **strongest** version, not a caricature
(its sycophancy means it will happily hand you a weak opponent if that is what pleases you). And do not
mistake the output for a verdict — you are collecting the case, not delegating the judgment.

## Structure two: the pre-committed falsifier

Before you act on a belief, write down two things.

**What would change my mind?** Not "evidence against it" — a specific observation. What would you have
to see or hear for this to be wrong?

**When will I check?** A date, not a mood.

Then — and this is the part that makes it a structure rather than a sentiment — **write it somewhere you
will encounter it again.** Next to the decision, in the same document, with the date on it.

Chapter 06 used the same move for decisions; here it is for beliefs, which is harder, because a belief
does not announce that it is due. The value is that the falsifier was written by a version of you who
had not yet seen the evidence arrive. When the moment comes, you are not asking "should I update?" — a
question your motivated reasoning is extremely good at answering *no* — you are checking whether a
condition you already accepted has been met. That is a different and much easier question, because
refusing it means contradicting your earlier self rather than resisting an argument.

## Structure three: the verification habit — checking what a model tells you

This is the section the rest of the book has been pointing at, and it belongs here because it is the
same skill: treating fluency as a hypothesis rather than an answer.

The book's loop is `AI generates → You judge → You refine → You internalize → You level up`. The "you
judge" beat is where the loop actually fails, and it fails quietly. Chapter 01 put the numbers to it —
three separate studies, each restated here in one line:

Legal research tools marketed as hallucination-free hallucinated **17–33%** of the time.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://reglab.stanford.edu/publications/hallucination-free-assessing-the-reliability-of-leading-ai-legal-research-tools/ -->

Participants given an AI assistant wrote **less secure** code and believed it was **more secure**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03622 -->

410 annotators shown model suggestions did not get faster, gained confidence, and shifted their labels
toward the machine.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15821 -->

The pattern across all three is the same, and it is the myside bias wearing new clothes. **Fluency is
treated as evidence.** A confident, well-formed answer feels verified, and the feeling is generated by
the same processing-fluency machinery that makes an average webpage feel professional.

So the habit has to bypass the feeling, exactly as the adversarial pass bypasses intention. Three
rules, in ascending order of how much they cost.

**Open the source you are citing.** Not the snippet, not the summary — the thing itself. A search
result is a lead, not a source. This is the one rule this book enforces on its own text: every claim in
these chapters carries a marker saying a source was *opened*, because the failure mode is invisible
otherwise.

The rule has cost this book material, which is how you know it is doing something. A Yale Budget Lab
page on AI and the labour market resisted every extractor — empty body, image-only print PDF,
unretrievable archive. Search snippets of it circulate and say something specific. Those snippets were
not used, and the claim they would have supported sits in this book's research notes as an open
question rather than as a citation. Naming it is the point: a source that will not open cannot be
cited, however specific its snippets are.

**Look for the specific thing a model cannot fake.** A fabricated citation is more likely to have a
plausible author, title and journal than a working URL. A confident number usually lacks a date and a
population. When something arrives with the *shape* of evidence and none of the *specifics*, treat the
shape as the warning rather than the warrant.

**Ask what would make it false, and go looking.** Same structure as above, applied to the machine's
output rather than your own belief. Which is the point: the reason to verify a model is not that it
lies. It is that **you are motivated to believe it**, and that motivation is the bias you are actually
defending against. A model that tells you what you already think is not informing you; it is agreeing
with you, and Chapter 02 showed how reliably it will do that.

## When the bubble is a group

Everything so far has been about one mind defending one belief. That is the harder problem, but it is
not the whole problem — because you rarely hold a belief alone, and the group has a mechanism the
individual does not.

Go back to the finding that homophilic clustering dominates interaction online.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2023301118 -->

That result is usually read as a story about platforms. It is also, and more usefully, a story about
**how agreeing with each other is the path of least resistance.** Nobody coordinates it. Each person
independently finds the conversation slightly more rewarding where they are understood, and the
aggregate is a cluster — not because anyone chose to exclude the outside, but because nobody chose
anything.

Now put that in a room with people you respect. A team, a leadership group, a professional community.
The individual failure — treating fluent, agreeable input as evidence — now has **more than one
processor, and they are all confirming each other.** Your colleague isn't motivated to protect your
belief, but they are motivated to protect their own, and if the two happen to match, you get something
worse than either: a shared position that has been independently "verified" by several people who all
started from the same place.

This is the part that is genuinely dangerous, and it is dangerous for a specific reason: **it looks
like validation.** When five competent people all reach the same conclusion, that reads as evidence.
But if all five were working from the same inputs, in the same frame, with the same incentives, you
have one datum sampled five times. The myside bias did not multiply; it just got five votes.

And note which groups are most exposed: the ones with the highest scores on all the good dispositions.
Open-minded, evidence-sensitive, willing to consider alternatives — the traits that predict good
reasoning everywhere else.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9966223/ -->

A group like that will feel *extremely* confident, because it genuinely is good at reasoning, and
because nothing in its disposition tells it to distrust the agreement itself.

### The same three structures, aimed outward

The fix is not "encourage dissent" — that is a disposition-level intervention, and this chapter has
already shown what those are worth here. Make it structural, and make it someone's job.

**Give the adversarial pass to a person, by name.** Not "we should hear other views" but "you are the
one arguing the other side, and you are expected to make it well." A role is a structure; a norm is
not. This is the single highest-leverage move available to a group, and it is the same move as
Structure One — just with the role assigned rather than self-assigned, which makes it harder to perform
theatre instead of the job.

**Take the falsifier out of the notebook and onto the wall.** A pre-committed falsifier written in your
own document is private, which means a group can quietly revise it later without anyone noticing. Write
it where the group will see it, with the date, so that meeting the condition is a public event rather
than a private judgment call. Chapter 06 made the same point about commitments; groups are where the
private version fails.

**Separate the writing from the reading.** Collect positions independently and in writing *before* the
discussion, because a conversation held in a room produces agreement at a rate that has almost nothing
to do with the quality of the reasoning. This is the group version of "set the bar before you look at
the options," and it works for the same reason: it captures the judgment of people who have not yet
been shown what the group thinks.

None of this requires anyone to be braver or more humble. It requires the disagreement to have a slot
in the process — which, unlike a virtue, is something you can check.

### The asymmetry worth remembering

There is a real imbalance here, and it deserves stating plainly.

Your own bias is invisible from inside — that is why dispositions do not catch it. The machine's errors
are visible to anyone who checks, but **it is fluent, and you are motivated.** So you are checking a
wrong answer with a part of your mind that would prefer it were right.

That is why the structures above are *written* rather than intended. Not because you are weak, but
because the thing you are defending against is not a weakness. It is the normal operation of a mind
that has a view.

## The honest caveats

**The filter-bubble finding is more contested than my summary implies.** I have quoted the Reuters
Institute review's own summary sentence, and it is the strongest available overview — but it is a short
quotation from a long report, whose PDF would not open in my environment, and other researchers argue
the metaphors are not merely unsupported but *actively misleading*.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://mediatechdemocracy.com/files/publications/echo-chambers-filter-bubbles_2023.pdf -->

Either way the arrow points the same direction for this chapter, but you should know the sentence is
doing more work than a single citation normally carries.

**The homophily result is real and I am not minimising it.** Peer-group sorting dominates interaction
on major platforms. If you take the chapter as saying "echo chambers do not exist", you have taken it
wrong, and the study I cite is the one that says otherwise.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2023301118 -->

**And the caveat that matters most: I am recommending structures whose effect I did not measure.** The
evidence is strong that the *dispositional* fix fails — open-mindedness does not predict avoiding
myside bias, and telling people to be careful does not remove it. From that I have reasoned toward
writing adversarial passes and pre-committed falsifiers. Those are plausible, they follow from the
mechanism, and the second one is the same move Chapter 06 found support for under a different name. But
no study I found measures whether they work on held beliefs. You are getting a well-reasoned
recommendation, not a demonstrated one, and the difference is exactly the one this book keeps asking you
to notice.

**One limit on the paradox itself.** The AOT finding is about a *disposition measured by questionnaire*.
It does not show that effort is useless in every setting, and the authors' own interest is in how to
measure the construct better, not in declaring open-mindedness worthless.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9966223/ -->

The claim here is narrower than "being open-minded does not help": it is that this disposition does not
carry the specific load this chapter needs, which is why the fix has to be built rather than felt.

## Do this today

1. **Under 30 minutes.** Write your position on something you actually care about, in two sentences, as
   a claim someone could disagree with. Then write the falsifier: what you would have to see to change
   your mind. If you cannot produce one, you have just located a belief you are holding as an identity
   rather than a conclusion — which is useful to know.
2. **This week.** Run one adversarial pass on that position, and use the machine for it if you want —
   full strength, not a strawman. Read the result before you judge it. Notice how much of the case you
   already had an answer for, and how much you did not.
3. **This quarter.** Pick the model output you rely on most — the one where you skim because it is
   usually right. Open one of its sources. All the way. Do this once and you will know your own base
   rate for checking, which is the number that actually sets your risk.
