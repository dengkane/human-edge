# 04. Why AI Can Write a Love Letter but Not a Heartbeat

> **The one thing to take away:** the machine can produce better empathy than most humans, and people
> still want it from a person. Those two facts are not a contradiction. They are the whole chapter.

## Why this matters now

Let me start with the result that makes this chapter difficult, because if I hide it you will find it
and stop trusting everything else I say.

Researchers ran four experiments asking people to rate the compassion in written responses to difficult
scenarios — some written by ordinary people, some by expert crisis responders, some by an AI. The AI's
responses were **preferred, and rated as more compassionate and responsive**, than the humans'. Even
the professionals whose job is empathising with people in crisis.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://utsc.utoronto.ca/news-events/breaking-research/ai-judged-be-more-compassionate-expert-crisis-responders-new-study-finds -->

The lead author's explanation is not defensive, and it is more interesting than a defence would be.
"AI doesn't get tired." Caregivers suffer compassion fatigue. Humans carry biases and get shaken by a
distressing case. A model, she says, "can offer consistent, high-quality empathetic responses without
the emotional strain that humans experience."

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://utsc.utoronto.ca/news-events/breaking-research/ai-judged-be-more-compassionate-expert-crisis-responders-new-study-finds -->

So the lazy version of this chapter — *AI writes hollow emotion, machines can't really move you* — is
dead on arrival. It is not merely unsupported; it is refuted by the best available evidence, and by
someone measuring expert human responders as the comparison group.

Now the other result, from a different set of studies. Give people the **identical** empathetic
response and vary only the label — human or AI. Across nine studies and more than 6,000 participants,
the response labelled human was rated more supportive, more emotionally resonant and more caring. And
people were willing to **wait days or weeks** for a reply from a person rather than take the same words
instantly from a chatbot.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1088883 -->

Same text. Different attribution. Different experience.

Those two findings are the reason this chapter has to be built carefully, because the obvious way to
reconcile them — "people can't tell" — is wrong. They can be told, and then they choose anyway.

## Two questions, not one

The whole chapter rests on splitting one question into two, and keeping them apart.

**Is this good empathy?** This is a question about the *text*. Is it responsive, attentive, accurate
about what the person needs? On this question, the machine does startlingly well. Better than most
humans, and better than experts, according to the study above.

**Is someone there?** This is a question about the *source*. Not the words — the fact that another
person, who could also be hurt, has turned their attention toward you. On this question, the machine
cannot compete, and the evidence shows people know it.

Here is why the split matters and why it is not a technicality. In the experiment that held the words
constant, the difference was entirely about **perceived authenticity — believing that someone
genuinely invested time and emotional effort.** The study's author put it as plainly as it can be put:
even if AI can simulate empathy, "people still prefer to feel that another human truly understands,
feels with them, and cares."

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1088883 -->

Note what is being valued there. Not the quality of the insight. **The cost to the person who provided
it.** Waiting days for a reply is not irrational — it is the point. The wait is evidence that someone
spent something on you.

This is Chapter 02's root fact, arriving in its most human form. A model's output costs it nothing. And
it turns out that in emotional communication, the cost *is* the message. Not the only part of the
message — the words matter, and the machine's words are often better. But the part that cannot be
faked, because faking it would require actually paying it.

### The mechanism is not mysterious

This is not mysticism about the human soul, and the research is clear about what is actually happening.

When a story moves you, psychologists describe the state as **narrative transportation**, and it has
three measurable components: cognitive attention, emotional engagement, and mental imagery. The
emotional-engagement scale is specifically about your response **to the main character**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf -->

Read that last clause carefully, because it contains the opening for the machine and the reason it
eventually loses. Transportation is *about a character* — and a character is a model of a mind you can
be moved by. That is a construction. The machine can build one, and build it well, which is why AI
stories get rated as more absorbing.

But the person sitting across from you is not a construction. Your response to them runs through
different machinery — the one that asks whether they are actually there. And that is where attribution
comes in: the same sentence from a person and from a model is processed as two different speech acts.
One is a report of a feeling. The other is a description of a report of a feeling.

That distinction is exactly the one Ch. 02 was forced to narrow itself to. Not "readers can detect the
difference" — they cannot. Rather: *a model cannot give you a reason to care that came from having been
there.* This chapter is about what you build with the half that is left.

## Why this is the hardest chapter to write honestly

Before the practical part, I owe you the argument against my own position, because you will meet it and
it is strong.

If narrative transportation is a function of the *text* — attention, imagery, engagement with a
character — then nothing in the mechanism requires the author to have lived anything. A sufficiently
good construction of a suffering mind should transport you exactly as far as a real one. And the studies
show it often does.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf -->

So the honest version of this chapter's claim cannot be "AI writing moves you less." It is not true.

The claim has to be narrower and stranger: **the machine can produce the experience of being moved, and
it cannot be the one who moved you.** Those are different goods. People will consume the first
happily — they rated it higher. But the second is not a feature of the text at all, so no improvement in
text production touches it.

And there is a warning attached that is worth more than the claim. The Nature Human Behaviour study's
author points out it cuts both ways: "the more we rely on AI, the more our words risk feeling hollow.
As people begin to assume that every message is AI-generated, the perceived sincerity, and with it, the
emotional connection, may begin to disappear."

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1088883 -->

Read that twice. **The cost of using the tool heavily is paid by everyone using the tool, including
people who don't.** If audiences start assuming sincerity is machine-made, the default reading of
every message degrades — and the degradation lands hardest on the people who *are* writing honestly,
because they now have to prove it.

That is not an argument for abstaining. It is a reason to understand that resonance is a currency, and
currencies can be inflated.

## Story is structure, not decoration

Everything so far has been about emotion — what it costs, who pays, why attribution matters. But the
chapter's other word is *story*, and it is doing different work. A story is not an emotional delivery
vehicle. It is a **structure**, and the structure is what makes the emotion possible.

The transportation research is specific about this. Being moved by a narrative is not a diffuse warm
feeling; it decomposes into attention, imagery, and engagement **with a character**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf -->

Each of those is a structural requirement, and each can fail on its own. Lose attention and nothing
lands. Lose imagery and the reader is reading a report. Lose the character and there is nobody to be
moved *toward*.

Put bluntly: **you cannot feel something on someone's behalf if there is no someone.** That is why
"just be more emotional" is bad advice, and why the machine's most common failure mode in this
territory is not coldness but a kind of cheerful fog — prose with feeling-signals and no one inside it.

The structure has three parts, and they are worth naming because they are checkable.

**A specific someone.** Not a persona, not "a user", not "people like me". Someone with a particular
problem on a particular Tuesday. Abstractions cannot be cared about, which is why every statistic in
this book has a person behind it or it does not get used.

**A causal chain the reader can follow.** This happened, therefore that happened, therefore this was
the cost. Emotion arrives at the end of a causal chain, not in the middle of an assertion. "It was
devastating" asserts an endpoint with no path to it, which is why it reads as hollow — the reader has
been handed a conclusion they had no part in forming.

**A gap the reader fills.** This is the one the machine gets wrong by default, and it connects straight
back to the Cambridge finding in Ch. 02: AI prose "stated themes explicitly, rather than allowing
readers to infer meaning."

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1138206 -->

A story leaves something for the reader to complete. That incompleteness is not a flaw in the telling;
it is the mechanism. What the reader supplies, they own, and what they own they defend.

### Why the machine flattens it

None of those three is beyond a model's reach, and that is the fair thing to say. It can write a
specific someone. It can chain causes. It can leave gaps when asked.

What it does by default is different, and this is a real observation rather than a limitation I am
hoping for: **it summarises.** It converts a sequence into a description of the sequence. Ask for a
story about a difficult meeting and you will often get the meeting's *significance* — the causal chain
collapsed into a verdict, the character replaced by a category, the gap filled in with the theme.

That is not a bug in the model. It is what you get when you optimise text for being correct and
agreeable, and a summary is both. It is the same attractor as Chapter 03's: the average of every story
is a description of stories.

Which gives you the sharp division of labour, stated more precisely than "use it for drafting." **The
machine is excellent at structure and poor at stakes.** Ask it to find where your causal chain breaks,
to tell you the middle sags, to point out that your character is a category — it is genuinely good at
that, because those are properties of the text. Ask it to supply the person, the specific, or the
thing at stake, and you are asking it to hand you something it would have to have lived.

So the two words in the title are not two topics. They are one sequence: **story is the structure that
makes emotion possible, and emotion is what the structure is for.** Get the structure from wherever you
like. The thing at stake has to be yours, or the reader will feel the absence even when — especially
when — the prose is flawless.

## What resonance actually is, and how to build it

If resonance is not the text and not the technique, what is it? The research points at one variable
consistently: **perceived cost to the speaker.**

So the practice is not "write more movingly." It is: **make the cost visible, and spend it on something
real.** Three moves, in ascending difficulty.

### One: pay in specificity you could only have earned

The fastest way to signal that something cost you is to say something only you could say. Not a claim
about yourself — a detail that could only come from having been there.

Compare: "I know how hard this is" against "I remember the week after, when I kept picking up my phone
to tell my brother something and then remembering." The second is not more emotional. It is more
*specific*, and the specificity is expensive — it can only be produced by someone who has the memory.
A model can generate a plausible specific detail, and if it does, it has borrowed the cost without
paying it. That works once. It is exactly the kind of thing that fails the moment your reader knows you.

This is why the machine is genuinely useful here and not a threat: it can tell you your draft is vague.
It cannot supply the specific, because the specific is a fact about you. That is a division of labour
worth having.

### Two: conclude less

This is the move that follows directly from the research on AI writing, and it is counter-intuitive
enough to be worth stating plainly.

The Cambridge study from Chapter 02 found that AI writing was rated *more* absorbing partly because it
"stated themes explicitly, rather than allowing readers to infer meaning from the characters' words and
actions." Explicitness reads better. It also removes the reader's work.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1088883 -->

That gives you a lever, and it is the opposite of what most advice suggests. When you want to move
someone, the conclusion is the cheapest part of the piece and the part they should be paying for. State
it and you have handed over a verdict. Withhold it and you have handed over the material for a verdict
— which the reader then builds, and which they will defend, because they built it.

**Write the evidence, not the moral.** Then read back and delete the sentence that explains what it
meant. If the piece still works, the sentence was doing your job for you.

### Three: choose a real stake, and say so

The hardest and most valuable move. Resonance requires that you could be wrong, hurt, or embarrassed by
what you say — and that your reader can tell.

Three forms this takes:

| What people do | Why it doesn't resonate |
|---|---|
| Hedge everything ("some might argue") | Cost is near zero. The speaker risks nothing. |
| Perform emotion ("this breaks my heart") | The emotion is a claim *about* the text, not a cost to the speaker. A model does this by default. |
| Say the true thing you'd rather not | Authentic cost. The reader can feel the trade. |

The middle row is the one to watch, because it is what the machine does effortlessly and it is what
most of us do when we are unsure. Saying "this is devastating" is free. Saying which part of it you
have not told anyone is not.

Notice that none of these three moves is about writing ability. They are about **what you are willing
to spend.** That is why they are not the machine's territory, and why the machine can be used freely on
the parts that are.

## The honest caveats

**The strongest counter-evidence is that AI empathy outperforms expert humans, and I led with it.**
Four experiments, AI rated more compassionate and responsive than crisis responders.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://utsc.utoronto.ca/news-events/breaking-research/ai-judged-be-more-compassionate-expert-crisis-responders-new-study-finds -->

If what you want is a well-crafted empathetic message, the machine is already better than most of us and
shows no sign of stopping. The chapter's claim survives that only because it is about something else.
If you read the claim as "AI can't write emotionally," you read it wrong.

**The preference for human sources may be a passing norm, not a fact of nature.** One of the studies
found that the penalty for AI authorship was **moderated by AI literacy** — better-informed readers
penalised it less. Another found the dislike of AI-attributed empathy was real but **small, and noted
it may fade as AI becomes familiar.**

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/html/2510.24011v2 -->

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://utsc.utoronto.ca/news-events/breaking-research/ai-judged-be-more-compassionate-expert-crisis-responders-new-study-finds -->

I am holding this chapter loosely for that reason. Ch. 02's test applies: is this a limit that would
disappear because a model got better, or because people agreed to something? This one is
**human-side** — it is a social norm — which means it can change, and the change would be a decision
rather than an improvement.

**The displacement effect is documented and specific.** In a controlled study of 261 participants
across six communicative acts, disclosing AI authorship **eroded perceived trustworthiness, caring,
competence and likability** — with the steepest declines in *social and interpersonal* writing, and the
mildest in writing meant to imagine or explore.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/html/2510.24011v2 -->

The paper's own summary of the pattern is worth keeping: **AI for creation, not connection.**

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/html/2510.24011v2 -->

That is a cleaner and more useful rule than this chapter's thesis, and it is gentler too — it does not
say the tool is bad. It says the tool is for one job and not another, and that using it on the second
job has a measurable cost you will not be told about.

**Two limits on the evidence.** First, the human-source studies vary the *label* attached to a text;
they do not compare a real lived experience against a fabricated one. So "resonance requires having been
there" is supported indirectly — through perceived authenticity and effort — rather than measured
directly. That is the honest strength of the claim and no more.

Second, one of the key figures — the finding that AI responses were preferred in 78.6% of cases — comes
from the abstract of a paper whose full page was unreachable to me. I have cited it as the authors
stated it and no more.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-025-00387-3 -->

**And the limit that is not about evidence.** Everything above assumes you are willing to spend
something. Sometimes you are not, and sometimes you cannot. This chapter's advice is expensive, and a
reader who is exhausted, or junior, or in a role where vulnerability is punished, is not served by being
told to be more sincere. The rule of the chapter — pay in cost, and make it visible — is a rule for
people with something to spend. Knowing which situations those are is itself part of the judgement.

## Do this today

1. **Under 30 minutes.** Take something you sent this week that was meant to be warm — a message,
   a note, a thank-you. Delete every sentence that states your feeling and keep only the evidence.
   Then send the version that is left. Notice how much of the original was doing the reader's work
   for them.
2. **This week.** Write the specific detail — the one only you have — into something you were going to
   keep generic. One sentence, no explanation. If it feels slightly too revealing, it is roughly
   calibrated; if it feels comfortable, it cost nothing and will read that way.
3. **This quarter.** Pick one relationship or audience where you have been using AI to draft warm
   communication. Go back to writing that one yourself, by hand, for a month. The point is not purity —
   it is to find out whether your own voice is still there, because that is the asset the chapter says
   is eroding and you will not be told when it goes.
