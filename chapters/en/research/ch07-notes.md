## What the research changed — the chapter inverted

The title promises "breaking out of the filter bubble." **The evidence says the filter bubble, as
popularly described, is not well supported** — and that finding made the chapter better rather than
breaking it.

The Reuters Institute's literature review is explicit: *"the work reviewed here suggests echo chambers
are much less widespread than is commonly assumed, [and] finds no support for the filter bubble"* claim.
Cinelli et al. do find homophilic clustering dominates interaction on Facebook and Twitter — so
*peer-group* sorting is real — but the strong version, that an algorithm quietly encloses an individual
in a personalised world they cannot see out of, is not what the data show.

So the lazy chapter — *"algorithms trap you in a bubble; delete the app"* — is dead. What replaces it is
harder and points at the reader:

**The pull toward agreement is internal, and it is not fixed by changing the information supply.** You do
not need a bubble to seek confirmation; it is the default. That is why Cinelli finds homophily in
*interaction* rather than in *exposure* — people encounter more diversity than they engage with.

And then the finding that reorganised the whole chapter, from Stanovich & Toplak:

> AOT scales are **potent predictors of performance on most rational thinking tasks**, and yet they
> **do not predict the avoidance of myside thinking — even though it is virtually the quintessence of
> the AOT concept.**

Read that again. The disposition everyone would prescribe — being open-minded, willing to consider
alternatives, postponing closure — predicts good reasoning on almost everything **except the one bias it
is named for**. "Try harder to be open-minded" is not a fix for the bias that matters most.

That is the chapter's spine, and it is the same shape as every other chapter in the book: the obvious
remedy is the one that does not work, and the real one is more specific.

## A source deliberately NOT used (and deliberately not marked)

The verification section names the **Yale Budget Lab** study on AI and the labour market
(<https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/evaluating-impact-ai-labor-market-current-state-affairs>) as an
example of the book's own rule in operation. It is **not** a source for this chapter and carries no
`verified` marker.

- It could not be opened in any form: empty body to every extractor, image-only print PDF,
  unretrievable Wayback capture (recorded in `ch01-notes.md`).
- Search snippets of it are specific and widely repeated. They were **not** used.
- **Why no marker:** a `verified` marker asserts that a claim was checked *against* the source. Here
  what I checked is that the source does not open. Attaching the marker to that would be exactly the
  misuse this chapter warns about, so the anecdote is carried in prose and this note instead.

## Note on cross-references

The verification section restates **three findings from Ch. 01** as separate one-line claims, each with
its own marker and its own source: RegLab (**7**), Perry et al. (**8**), Schroeder et al. (**9**). All
three were **opened and verified during Ch. 01's research** and are cited here by their own URLs, not
bundled under one. They are restatements, not re-verifications — the markers point at the URLs checked
in Ch. 01, and the numbers match that chapter's text exactly (17–33%; less secure / more secure; 410
annotators).

**First draft error, caught before publish:** these three findings were originally bundled into a single
sentence under a single RegLab marker. That would have attributed Perry et al.'s and Schroeder et al.'s
results to the RegLab source — a `verified` marker doing work for claims whose source it does not cover,
which is precisely the failure this chapter warns about.

## Open questions

- **The Reuters conclusion is quoted from the review's own summary text, not its PDF body** — the PDF
  would not extract in this environment. The sentence is short, specific and appears verbatim in the
  review's own summary, but it is a one-line citation of a long report, and the chapter treats it as
  such rather than as a detailed finding.
- **Does structure genuinely beat disposition for myside bias?** The chapter recommends structural
  fixes (adversarial passes, written falsifiers) because the dispositional fix is documented to fail.
  But I did not find a study measuring whether those particular structures work. The advice is reasoned
  from the failure of the alternative, which is weaker than direct evidence and is flagged in the
  caveats.
- **Ch. 12 must not re-teach this chapter** (per the boundary note): when Ch. 12 is written, the
  *habit of maintaining practices* over years — decay, plateaus — must be the new material, with this
  chapter's techniques assumed rather than restated.

## Claims downgraded or dropped

- **"The clustering is in engagement, not in the information supply."** Caught in review and corrected. This
  was my inference from the homophily result, not what the paper claims; Cinelli treats information-diffusion bias
  as a co-equal ingredient. Downgraded to a framing of the problem rather than a measured split.
- **"In this book's own research, the coverage of a paywalled article contradicted its snippet."** Dropped —
  I could not point to the specific instance, and an undocumented anecdote about my own rigour is the worst
  possible thing to assert in a section about verification. Replaced with the Yale Budget Lab case, which *is*
  documented in `ch01-notes.md` (source would not open; snippets deliberately not cited).
- **"Algorithms seal you in a personalised bubble."** Dropped as unsupported in its strong form.
  Replaced with the distinction between *exposure* and *engagement*.
- **"Be more open-minded and you will reason better."** Downgraded sharply — AOT predicts good
  reasoning on most tasks but **not** on myside bias, the case this chapter is about.
- **"Filter bubbles cause polarisation."** Softened: polarisation is documented; the bubble mechanism
  is not the established cause.
- **"Critical-thinking training fixes this."** Not claimed; the transfer evidence is thin and the one
  study found reports improvements without transfer.
