# Research notes — Ch. 04: Story & Emotion

The chapter delivers the **no experience** blind spot as a capability: **resonance** — why anyone should
care. It must build on the *narrowed* claim Ch. 02 was forced into, not the wide one the Cambridge
*Bot or not* study refuted. Ch. 02 says it explicitly:

> blind spot two is narrower than it first sounds — *a model cannot give you a reason to care that came
> from having been there*

Ch. 04's job is to turn that sentence into a practice. The boundary note in `chapters/en/README.md` sets
the seam with Ch. 03: *03 is taste (what is worth making); 04 is resonance (why anyone should care).
Taste selects; emotion connects.*

## What the research changed

**I expected the evidence to say "AI empathy is detected as fake." It says the opposite twice over, and
then says something more interesting.**

Three separate lines of research, all peer-reviewed:

1. AI-written empathetic responses were rated **more compassionate** than those from expert human crisis
   responders (four experiments).
2. In a follow-up, AI empathy was judged higher in quality — **but people still chose to receive empathy
   from a human**.
3. With empathy attributed to a human vs an AI, the *identical* text was rated more supportive and more
   emotionally resonant when labelled human, across nine studies and 6,000+ participants.

Those three cannot be reconciled by "readers can tell." They can only be reconciled by separating two
questions the chapter now keeps apart: **is this good empathy?** (the machine often wins) versus **is
someone there?** (the machine cannot be). The chapter is built on that split, and it is stronger than
the "AI can't do emotion" version because it does not depend on readers detecting anything.

## Counter-evidence

| Source | What it contradicts | Response |
|--------|--------------------|----------|
| **Ovsyannikova et al. 2025** — <https://utsc.utoronto.ca/news-events/breaking-research/ai-judged-be-more-compassionate-expert-crisis-responders-new-study-finds> | AI empathy beat **expert human crisis responders** on compassion ratings. Directly undermines "a machine cannot move you." | The chapter's central concession, and it is *why* the chapter splits into two questions. The machine wins on the text; it loses on the source. Their own explanation ("AI doesn't get tired") is more interesting than a defence, and the chapter uses it. |
| **Wenger et al. 2026** — <https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-025-00387-3> | AI rated higher in quality in 78.6% of cases. | Adopted as the chapter's spine: quality and source are separable, and people separate them. |
| **Nakano et al. 2026** (IUI) | Disclosure effects are **moderated by AI literacy** — higher literacy mitigates or reverses the penalty. | The chapter says the preference is not a law of nature; it is a social norm, and norms move. That weakens "the machine can never resonate" and strengthens the claim that resonance is a *decision about who is speaking*. |
| **Green & Appel 2024** | Transportation is a function of the **text** (imagery, character, attention), not of the author's biography. | This is the honest problem with the chapter's thesis. The chapter must show that author is *also* an input — which the Rubin and Nakano studies do, since both hold the text constant and change only attribution. Stated plainly in the caveats. |
| **Ovsyannikova et al.** (AI-aversion note) | The preference **shifted slightly** when the source was revealed as AI — i.e. the penalty may fade with familiarity. | Recorded as a reason to hold the claim loosely, consistent with Ch. 02's rule about machine-side vs human-side limits. |

## Open questions

- **Is the human-source preference stable or a transitional norm?** Nakano et al. find AI literacy erodes it; Ovsyannikova et al. found the penalty was small and may fade. If it disappears, the chapter's advice changes from "be the one who was there" to something else. This is the chapter's biggest exposure and it is stated in the caveats.
- **No study I found holds text constant *and* varies whether the author actually lived the experience** — the Rubin design varies the *label*, not the biography. So the strongest form of "resonance requires having been there" is supported indirectly. Flagged.
- **Wenger's 78.6% is read from the indexed abstract**, not the paper page (Nature blocked). If it matters, verify at source.

## Claims downgraded or dropped

- **"Readers can tell AI writing is emotionally hollow."** Dropped — the evidence says the opposite in three separate studies. Replaced with the quality/source split.
- **"AI cannot produce moving text."** Dropped. It can, demonstrably, and sometimes better than experts.
- **"Empathy requires lived experience, therefore AI empathy is worthless."** Downgraded to the narrow, defensible claim: *the value people place on empathy depends on who it comes from*, which holds text constant.
- **"The preference for human empathy will persist."** Not claimed. Recorded as a norm that may be moving.
