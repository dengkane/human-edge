# 03. Taste

> **The one thing to take away:** the average is an attractor that both you and the machine fall into,
> and taste is the deliberate act of leaving it.

A writer in India is typing about her favourite actor. She gets as far as the letter **S** — Shah
Rukh Khan — and the writing assistant offers to finish it for her.

*Shaquille O'Neal.*

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2025/04/ai-suggestions-make-writing-more-generic-western -->

In the same experiment, 118 participants from the US and India wrote about culturally grounded topics.
Some had the assistant, some did not. Writers who mentioned a favourite food got suggested pizza.
Writers describing a holiday got suggested Christmas.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2025/04/ai-suggestions-make-writing-more-generic-western -->

That "S" is the whole chapter in one keystroke. The assistant is not being stupid. It is doing exactly
what it was built to do: reach for the most likely next word. And the most likely next word, in a
model trained mostly on English text, is the one a billion people have typed before.

This chapter is about what that costs you, and what you can build to resist it.

It starts earlier than you would expect — before AI, before the model, before any of this.

## The web got boring without any help from AI

In 2018, a researcher set out to do something patient and slightly obsessive. He collected more than
227,000 screenshots of about 10,000 popular websites, spanning 2003 to 2019, and used computer vision
to measure how alike they looked, year by year.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.jonkolko.com/phd/writing/25-07-12-homogenization-of-web-design -->

The result is a curve that bends the wrong way. Websites got *less* alike until about 2007. Then the
trend reversed hard. Between 2010 and 2019, the average layout distance between sites **fell 44%**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.jonkolko.com/phd/writing/25-07-12-homogenization-of-web-design -->

Remember the date. There was no generative AI in that window. No model, no prompt, no diffusion. The
web converged anyway.

The designers he interviewed named the causes themselves: shared frameworks, responsive design
collapsing every layout onto the same stackable columns, CMS templates, and SEO rules dictating what
sits above the fold. And one finding should stop you cold. **Adopting the same libraries correlated
strongly with looking the same.**

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.jonkolko.com/phd/writing/25-07-12-homogenization-of-web-design -->

I am opening a chapter about taste with a study about sameness *before* AI because the easy version of
this chapter is a lie — and a flattering one.

The easy version goes like this: AI has no taste, that is why everything looks the same now, and your
job is to be the human who still has taste.

Every part of that is either false or useless. The sameness started long before the machines. And "be
the human with taste" is not advice. It is a compliment you are hoping to deserve.

Here is the real problem, and it is harder because it is not about the machine at all.

**You and the model share an attractor.** You both drift toward what is familiar, fluent, and typical,
because that is what people reward. Chapter 02 showed that the model has no preference of its own — it
lands on the centre of everything it ever read. This chapter is about the discovery that the centre is
not a machine thing. It is where human judgment goes to rest.

Which means taste is not a gift you were born with. It is a discipline you practise or you don't.

### Why the average is so hard to resist

Before you can decide whether you have taste, you need to see why it is so easy to lose.

The answer is older than design and has nothing to do with computers.

Psychologists call it **processing fluency**. The finding is decades deep and boringly consistent: the
more fluently you can process something, the more you like it. Aesthetic pleasure is a function of how
your mind handles the object, not of some property the object has.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15582859/ -->

Read that as a description of your own taste, and it should make you a little uncomfortable.

What feels like *"I know good work when I see it"* is, at least partly, *"this was easy for me to
parse."* Prototypes get rated more attractive. Familiar layouts convert better. The template that
"feels professional" feels that way because it looks like the last hundred things you trusted.

That explains the pre-AI convergence with no need for laziness or conspiracy theories.

A designer A/B tests two layouts. The more familiar one wins, because visitors read it faster. A
founder picks the design that feels safe. Do that across every design team for a decade, and you get a
44% collapse in visual variety — **from nothing more sinister than humans preferring what they can
process easily.**

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.jonkolko.com/phd/writing/25-07-12-homogenization-of-web-design -->

Now add the machine, and be precise about what it adds.

A model samples from a probability distribution. The densest mass sits near the mode — the most
typical thing in its training data. Train it on human preferences, and you have built a machine that
optimises for immediate human approval. Chapter 02 called the result the Artificial Hivemind:
individual models repeat themselves, and different models produce strikingly similar output.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/121421 -->

Here is the sentence to keep. **The same bias that made human-built websites converge through A/B
tests has been distilled into a reward function and industrialised.**

It is beauty-in-averageness with a training budget. The pull you feel toward the fluent, safe option is
real. It is now also the thing with the most compute behind it in the history of design.

## The cost shows up in the crowd, never in your own work

Now the finding that should worry you most. It comes from the best study in this chapter.

Researchers gave some writers story ideas from a language model and gave others nothing. The
AI-assisted stories were rated **more creative, better written, and more enjoyable**. The biggest gains
went to the *least* creative writers.

If the study stopped there, it would be a happy story about AI lifting everyone up.

It did not stop there. **The AI-assisted stories were measurably more similar to each other than the
stories written by humans alone.**

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532/ -->

The authors called it a social dilemma, and their wording beats anything I could write: *with
generative AI, writers are individually better off, but collectively a narrower scope of novel content
is produced.*

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532/ -->

Sit with the trap in that sentence.

Every single decision to use the tool is *rational*. Your draft gets better. You finish faster. There
is no moment where you feel a loss, because the loss is spread across everyone and only shows up in
the crowd. Nobody's story got worse. Every story got a little more like every other story.

That is why "just make good work" is not a strategy. You will not feel the drift from inside it.

Your own output keeps improving. Your *distinctiveness* — the thing that made you worth choosing over
a competent stranger — quietly slides toward the average.

And the slide has a direction.

Back to that experiment in India and the US. Indian participants kept **25%** of the assistant's
suggestions; Americans kept 19%. The Indian writers also had to correct far more of them — so their
productivity gain was *smaller*.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2025/04/ai-suggestions-make-writing-more-generic-western -->

The average is not neutral. It is the average of whoever did the training. Which means the centre is
somebody's specific taste wearing the costume of universality.

## What taste actually is

So taste is not a gift, and the average pulls on you and the machine alike.

What are you supposed to build?

The honest answer: taste is **a position you can defend and revise**. Both halves carry weight.

A *defensible* position is one where you can say why. Not "I like it" — why *this* over *that*, in
words that survive being said out loud to someone who disagrees. If your reason collapses the moment
someone raises an eyebrow, you had a preference, not a judgment.

Chapter 02's sycophancy findings are the warning here. A model agrees with whoever is talking. If your
reasoning does the same thing, you have not escaped the average — you have just made it local.

A *revisable* position is one that evidence can change. This is the half people miss when they confuse
taste with stubbornness. Having taste does not mean holding the line against all objection. It means
your position has reasons, and reasons can be attacked — and can lose.

Together, the two are stricter than they sound. A position that cannot be defended is a mood. A
position that cannot be revised is an identity. **Taste is the narrow band between them, and most of
the work is staying in it.**

Here is a test, drawn straight from the fluency finding. When you look at a piece of work — yours or
someone else's — ask which of these you are actually responding to:

| What you might be feeling | What it usually is |
|---|---|
| "This is clean" | Fluency. You parsed it easily. Often a real virtue, sometimes an excuse for thin work. |
| "This is professional" | Typicality. It looks like what you have seen rewarded. |
| "This feels wrong" | Fluency failing. Sometimes hard work, sometimes just unfamiliarity — check which. |
| "I can say why it works" | The start of a judgment. Not proof, but the only place one starts. |

The first three are not worthless. Fluency is real, and audiences do reward it.

The point is that the first three happen *to* you automatically. Only the fourth is something you
produce.

### Taste is connective

There is a second half the "reject the average" framing misses — and it is the part that makes taste
useful instead of just critical.

Taste is not only the ability to say no. It is the ability to notice that **two things belong together
because they share a quality** — and to see it before anyone else puts them side by side.

Watch yourself do it and you will find it is not a comparison. You are not running down a checklist.
You are noticing a likeness. This paragraph has the same shape as that one. This hire answers the
question the project was really asking. This colour is doing the job the silence was doing two pages
back. The two things differ in content and match in *quality*, and the recognition arrives before you
can explain it.

That wordless arrival is why taste gets mistaken for magic — and why the fluency trap is so seductive.
Both are fast. Both feel like knowing.

The difference is what happens next. Fluency stops at "this is pleasing." Taste keeps going: it says
*these two belong together*. That is a claim. It can be wrong. And you can test it by putting them
together and seeing whether the likeness holds.

This is why taste has to be practised inside **one domain**, at least at first. The connective sense
is trained by exposure within a field. You learn what a well-made thing in *your* medium looks like,
and the likenesses you can feel are likenesses among those things. That is this chapter. Carrying a
structure from one field into a completely different one is a separate skill, and it is Chapter 05's.

The machine is not useless here, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. It is very good at
retrieving things that are *nominally* similar — same genre, same keywords, same category — and that
can hand you raw material.

What it cannot do is tell you the likeness is *worth* anything. That needs knowing what the category
is *for*. A model can tell you two paintings share a palette. It cannot tell you the shared palette is
why both are worth an afternoon. That judgment is yours, and it is the one that gets tested when you
put them together and someone says "so what?"

Which gives you the sharper version of the practice. **Collect things you believe belong together, and
write down the quality they share.** Not the category — the quality.

If you cannot name it, you have a grouping, not a taste. If you can, you have something you can use: a
standard that lets you spot the next one, in a form someone else can argue with.

### Where taste is built

If taste is a position with reasons that can be revised, it gets built the way any reasoned position
does: make claims, watch them fail, adjust. That is a practice, and every practice has a shape.

**First: commit in public, at low stakes.** Not "this is good" in your own head — say it where someone
can respond. Not humility theatre. An unstated judgment cannot be falsified, and an unfalsifiable
judgment cannot improve. State the reason, not just the verdict.

**Second: seek work that contradicts your fluency.** This is the hard one, because fluency is
pleasurable and you are wired to chase it. The practical version: deliberately spend time with work
you dislike that competent people defend. If you cannot find the argument for it, you have learned
something about the limits of your own reasoning.

**Third: separate the judgment from the outcome.** A recruiter once told me she had learned to distrust
the candidate she liked most in the first minute. Not because the reaction was wrong — because it was
not information. Good work can fail commercially. Bad work can succeed. If your taste is calibrated
only to outcomes, it is not taste. It is hindsight.

There is a fourth move, and it earns its own warning rather than a bullet.

The **design fixation** research found that seeing generated output narrows what designers produce
afterwards. Generative AI shows the same fixation humans do, and creators who absorb the patterns start
prompting for more of the same — feeding the convergence.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05870 -->

The upshot is blunt: **generate wide, choose narrow.**

Use the machine for volume, because volume is what it is good at. Then make the distinctive choice
yourself — with reasons. The study that found the individual gain and the collective loss is clear that
the model raises the floor. The ceiling is not in the model. It is in whoever does the curating.

That reframes what the tool is for. Use it to pick your answer, and you have outsourced the only part
that was ever yours. Use it to see the whole space of answers, then reject most of them on stated
grounds, and you have used it to make your taste *sharper* — because you are now rejecting a hundred
plausible things instead of the three you would have thought of alone.

## The honest caveats

**The strongest counter-evidence to this chapter is that AI made individual work better.** In the
Science Advances experiment, AI-assisted stories were rated more creative, better written and more
enjoyable, with the largest gains for the least creative writers.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532/ -->

If you are a strong writer, that finding is about the people you compete with. It should worry you more
than any claim about machines replacing you. The floor is rising. Your whole defence is the ceiling —
which is exactly the thing this chapter says you have to build yourself.

**But I have to be honest about a gap in the evidence.** I found solid research on the *mechanism* —
fluency makes the average attractive. And solid research on the *failure mode* — AI-assisted work
converges. What I did not find is a peer-reviewed study measuring whether taste-specific training
actually helps anyone leave the average. The practice above is reasoned from the mechanism, not cited
from an experiment. Treat it as a hypothesis with a plausible foundation — weaker than the rest of
this book's claims.

**The web-convergence figure is second-hand here.** The 44% decline is quoted from a design scholar's
close reading of the Goree et al. paper, because the ACM page itself was unreachable to me and I will
not cite a number I could not verify at source.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.jonkolko.com/phd/writing/25-07-12-homogenization-of-web-design -->

The paper is real, and the number appears the same way in the summary's quotation of the authors'
conclusion. But you should know the chain.

**The design-fixation finding is a preprint**, not peer-reviewed, and its framing partly clashes with
the Goree result: it treats fixation as a property of generative AI, while the web study shows the same
convergence with no AI in the room.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05870 -->

I have used it as a mechanism, not as proof that machines cause the convergence.

**One limit on the fluency research.** Reber and colleagues describe aesthetic pleasure as a function
of processing fluency — but I could only open the abstract, not the full paper, so the claim here is
stated no more strongly than the abstract supports.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15582859/ -->

**And the uncomfortable structural point.** Chapter 02's own test asks whether a limit would vanish
because a model improved or because people agreed to something. Taste passes that test — it is a claim
about who decides what is good, not about model capability.

But the same test exposes how *conditional* taste is on a culture that keeps rewarding it. If every
incentive keeps pointing at the fluent choice, taste is a position you have to pay for again and
again. This chapter's advice assumes you are willing to. That is a real assumption. Not everyone is in
a position to pay.

## Do this today

1. **Under 30 minutes.** Take three pieces of work in your field you consider good and one you consider
   mediocre. Write one sentence per piece saying *why*, using terms someone could disagree with. If
   your reasons for the good three all reduce to "it feels right", you have found the edge of your
   taste.
2. **This week.** Find a piece of work you dislike that competent people defend. Write the strongest
   case for it you can — properly, not as a strawman. If you cannot, that is information about the
   limits of your own judgment, which is worth having.
3. **This quarter.** Pick one recurring decision you make (what to publish, what to hire, what to
   build). For a month, write down your judgment *and* your reason *before* you learn the outcome.
   Then check the reasons, not the outcomes. Reasons that keep being wrong are the ones to revise.
   Reasons that keep being right are the start of a position you can defend.
