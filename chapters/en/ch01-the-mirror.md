# 01. The Mirror

> **The one thing to take away:** there are two kinds of knowing — one you can look up, and one you
> have to earn — and AI just repriced them. The first got cheap. The second got scarce.

## Why this matters now

In February 2020, if you wanted a job writing software, the market was yours. Tech postings were
abundant, entry-level roles existed, and the path was legible: learn the language, build a portfolio,
get hired, learn on the job, become senior.

By July 2025, software engineer postings were down 49% from that February 2020 baseline, and several
specific developer titles — Android, Java, .NET, iOS, web — were down more than 60%.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.hiringlab.org/2025/07/30/the-us-tech-hiring-freeze-continues/ -->

Here is the part almost nobody quotes: **nearly half of that decline had already happened before
ChatGPT was released.**

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.hiringlab.org/2025/07/30/the-us-tech-hiring-freeze-continues/ -->

That single fact is the reason this book starts here rather than with a triumphant story about AI. If
you are reading this because you feel like the ground moved under your feet, you are not imagining it
— but you are probably misdiagnosing why. The ground did move. AI is not the only thing that moved it.
And the skills you are worried about losing may not be the ones that actually went.

This chapter is the mirror. Not the flattering kind, and not the cruel kind either. A mirror shows you
what is actually there, which is usually more specific than either your hope or your fear. Which parts
of what you know are worth defending? Which parts were never really yours? And — the question that
matters most — how do you tell the difference before the market tells you?

The stakes are concrete. Workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations are now employed
about **19% below** where they would be had they kept pace with their less-exposed peers, with no
comparable gap for experienced workers.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/ -->

A separate Census Bureau analysis of administrative data found early-career workers (22–24) in the
most AI-exposed industries declining about **12%** over the ten quarters following ChatGPT's release.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-27.html -->

Note what those numbers are *not* saying. They are not saying the professions are dying. Bureau of
Labor Statistics projections still have software developers growing **17.9%** between 2023 and 2033,
far faster than the 4.0% average across all occupations.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2025/ai-impacts-in-bls-employment-projections.htm -->

The field is growing. The *door* is narrowing. Those two facts are not in tension, and holding both at
once is the first thing this book asks of you.

## Two kinds of knowing, and only one of them got cheap

The useful distinction is old, but AI gave it teeth. Economists separate **codified knowledge** —
established information you could write in a textbook, procedure, or checklist — from **tacit
knowledge**, the understanding you get from doing the thing, failing, and adjusting.

The Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas put it directly: if a model can replicate codified knowledge but
not tacit knowledge, then AI will **automate** jobs that lean on textbook knowledge while it
**complements** jobs that demand experiential judgment.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0224 -->

This is not a claim that AI is bad at things. It is a claim about *which* things it changes the price
of. And it explains a pattern that otherwise looks contradictory: employment for young workers in
exposed occupations is falling while wages in those same sectors rise. Nationally, nominal average
weekly wages rose 7.5% since fall 2022; in computer systems design they rose **16.7%**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0224 -->

Both at once, because the *composition* of who gets hired changed, not the value of the work.

Consider what a graduate actually brings to a first job. They arrive with the codified half of a
discipline: syntax, definitions, standard patterns, the textbook answer. That is precisely the part
that is now instant and free. For an entry-level employee, those textbook tasks *are* the expert part
of the job — the thing they were hired to do.

For someone with fifteen years of experience, the same tasks are the *inexpert* part of their work,
the part they would happily hand off. Dallas Fed's analysis makes this asymmetry explicit: a model can
substitute for entry-level workers who have book-learning but no experience, while complementing
experienced workers whose judgment cannot be replicated.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0224 -->

The market's behaviour matches. Among software and tech postings, the share requiring **5+ years** of
experience rose from 37% to 42% between mid-2022 and mid-2025, while the share asking for 2–4 years
fell from 46% to 40%. In occupations that were *not* part of the tech freeze, the same 5+ requirement
actually **fell**, from 16% to 11%.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.hiringlab.org/2025/07/30/experience-requirements-have-tightened-amid-the-tech-hiring-freeze/ -->

Read those last two sentences together, because that comparison is the whole chapter. Across the
economy, employers were loosening experience requirements. In the AI-exposed corner of it, they were
tightening them. The market did not conclude that experience is worthless. It concluded that
experience is what it cannot get cheaply.

**Here is the honest limit of that finding, and this book will keep doing this:** none of the research
finds that *pay* shifted toward judgment. Dallas Fed's data show no relationship between a field's AI
exposure and its post-2022 wage growth once you account for the experience premium. The adjustment
showed up in employment, not in salaries.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0224 -->

If you were hoping this chapter would tell you that being wise now pays more, it will not. What it
will tell you is narrower and more useful: **the composition of who gets hired changed, and it
changed in favour of the thing you cannot look up.**

### Why "it's just book learning" undersells the problem

It is tempting to hear "codified knowledge got cheap" and shrug. You were never just memorising, you
tell yourself. You understand things.

Maybe. But the test is not what you believe about your understanding. It is whether your output could
have been produced by someone who only read about it. And the research on what happens when people
use these tools suggests the gap between "understanding" and "assembling the plausible answer" is
narrower than most of us assume — including for experts.

Sixteen experienced open-source developers were asked to forecast how much faster AI would let them
complete real tasks on repositories they knew well. They predicted a 24% speed-up. Economists
predicted 39%. The measured result was **19% slower.**

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089 -->

The developers were not stupid and they were not novices. They were wrong about their own work in a
specific direction: they mistook the *feeling* of fluency for the fact of it. That mistake is the
subject of this book, and it is the reason the next section is a test rather than a pep talk.

Participants in another study who used an AI assistant wrote **significantly less secure code** — and
were **more likely to believe they had written secure code**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03622 -->

In a third, 410 annotators given model suggestions did not get faster, reported higher confidence, and
"strongly took the LLM suggestions, significantly changing the label distribution" — the human in the
loop rubber-stamped the output rather than checking it.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15821 -->

That last finding is the uncomfortable one. "A human reviews it" is the standard reassurance offered
for AI output, and it is often not a check at all. It is a signature.

## The mirror: what the market stopped paying for

If codified knowledge got cheap, the honest question is which of your own skills were closer to
codified than you thought. Not which ones you *could* defend in an argument — which ones your actual
daily output depended on.

The pattern in the data is that the exposed work is the work that can be specified in advance. The BLS
projections make this unusually legible, because they show which tasks the agency expects AI to take
over and which it expects to create demand for.

Credit analysts are projected to decline 3.9%; claims adjusters 4.4%; insurance appraisers 9.2%. At the
same time, software developers grow 17.9%, personal financial advisors 17.1%, and database architects
10.8%.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2025/ai-impacts-in-bls-employment-projections.htm -->

The pattern is not "technical good, non-technical bad" — lawyers grow 5.2%, paralegals only 1.2%.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2025/ai-impacts-in-bls-employment-projections.htm -->

The pattern is **specifiable versus judgmental**. A claims adjuster's job can largely be described as
rules applied to inputs. So can a paralegal's document review. A financial advisor's job is mostly
what happens when the rules run out and a human being is frightened about their future — which is why
"robo-advisors" have competed for years and human advisors are still projected to grow 17.1%.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2025/ai-impacts-in-bls-employment-projections.htm -->

This gives you a way to audit your own work that has nothing to do with job titles. Take the tasks you
did last week. For each one, ask: **could a competent stranger produce this from a written
description, without ever having done it?** If yes, it is codified, and it is on the block. If no —
if producing it required something you only know because you were there, or because you have seen how
this goes wrong — that is tacit, and that is where your value now sits.

Be honest about the ratio. Most of us will find more codified work in our week than we expected, and
that discovery is the point of the mirror. It is information, not a verdict.

### Why the mirror is more accurate than it used to be

The reason to take this seriously now, rather than as a general observation about technology, is that
the failure is no longer hypothetical and no longer invisible to the client.

Legal research tools sold as "hallucination-free" by their vendors hallucinate **17% to 33%** of the
time, according to the first preregistered evaluation of them. The vendors' claims were, in the
researchers' words, overstated.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://reglab.stanford.edu/publications/hallucination-free-assessing-the-reliability-of-leading-ai-legal-research-tools/ -->

A survey of 319 knowledge workers found that the more confident someone was in generative AI, the
**less** critical thinking they applied — and that the nature of the thinking shifted toward
verification, integration, and oversight of the tool.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/ -->

Notice what that second finding implies. The premium is not disappearing. It is *moving* — from
producing the first draft to judging it. And judging output you did not produce is a harder skill than
producing it, not an easier one, because you have not been through the process that would tell you
where it is wrong.

That is the mirror's real message. The skill that got cheap is production. The skill that got scarce
is the ability to tell good production from bad when both look fluent. And the second one cannot be
acquired by reading about it, which is exactly why the rest of this book is about practice.

## The test you can run on yourself

Everything above is a claim about the labour market. This section is about you, and it is designed to
produce an answer you will not like.

Here is the test. Pick the thing you are best at — the skill you would name if someone asked what you
bring. Now ask three questions in order.

**First: can it be specified?** Write a description of how you do it, detailed enough that a
competent stranger could follow it. Not a summary — the actual procedure. If you can write it down
completely, you have just demonstrated that it is codified, and you have also discovered the thing
you may soon be competing against.

**Second: where did it come from?** Did you get it from a course, a book, a tutorial, a standard
practice? Or did you get it from a specific failure you still remember — the client who reacted badly,
the system that broke at 2am, the estimate that was wrong for a reason you only understood afterwards?
Codified knowledge can be learned in any order, by anyone, at any time. Tacit knowledge has a
biography. It arrived in a sequence, and the sequence is why you have it.

**Third — the one that actually discriminates: would a model have known to ask?** Not "could a model
answer this". Could it have known the question mattered? The experienced developer knows that the
obvious solution will not survive contact with the third use case, and cannot always say why. That
"cannot always say why" is the signal, not a weakness. If you can fully articulate your judgment, it
is closer to a rule than a judgment.

Most people find their skill lands in an uncomfortable place: partly codified, partly not, with the
codified part being the part they were proudest of, because codified knowledge is *demonstrable*.
Nobody compliments you for judgment you cannot show. They compliment you for the deliverable.

The Dallas Fed's experience-premium data gives this an economic shape. The median occupation has a 40%
gap between entry-level and experienced pay, rising above 100% for lawyers, underwriters, and credit
analysts. The occupations with the highest AI exposure are, on average, the ones with the **highest**
experience premiums.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0224 -->

That is the mirror at its most precise. The market is not rewarding the field. It is pricing the
distance between someone who knows the procedure and someone who knows what the procedure is for.

### What the mirror does not say

It does not say to abandon your field, or to become a manager, or that technical skills are worthless
— the strongest growth projections in the BLS data are technical.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2025/ai-impacts-in-bls-employment-projections.htm -->

It does not say the professions are shrinking; most are growing.

And it does not say experience is *safe*. The gap in the Stanford data is between age 22–25 and older
workers — it is silent about what happens to a 45-year-old whose own judgment has been quietly
outsourced to a tool for five years, which is the subject of Chapter 11.

What the mirror says is narrow and actionable: **the part of your skill that is a procedure is now
cheap, and the part that is a decision is now the thing you are paid for.** Chapter 02 goes on to ask
what, exactly, machines cannot do — the four blind spots that make the second half of that sentence
structural rather than temporary. You need that answer before you can decide what to build.

## The honest caveats

**This chapter's central evidence is contested, and you should know how.** The Stanford findings are
described by their own authors as descriptive, not causal estimates; the employment gap shrinks when
you control for education, and some of the divergent trends predate generative AI.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/ -->

The Census analysis attributes up to a quarter of the early-career decline to monetary-policy shocks.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-27.html -->

So "AI caused this" is not established. "This happened, and AI is a leading explanation" is.

**Strong counter-evidence exists, and I am not hiding it.** A study of 25,000 Danish workers found
effects on earnings and hours that were precisely estimated to be null — ruling out anything larger
than about 2% two years after ChatGPT. Employers absorbed AI mainly through task reorganisation, not
layoffs.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777 -->

Census data show only **2%** of firms reporting AI-related employment decreases, and 66% of AI users
using it solely to augment their work.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-25.html -->

**The strongest counter-example to the whole thesis** comes from customer support: AI raised
productivity 14% on average, but **34% for novice and low-skilled workers**, with minimal impact on
experienced ones. In that setting, AI *compressed* the experience premium instead of raising it.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161 -->

I take that seriously. It means the pattern in this chapter is not a law of nature; it depends on the
work. Where the job is mostly codified, AI helps novices catch up. Where judgment dominates, it does
not. Support work is near one end of that scale, which is why the finding is real and also why it does
not generalize for free.

**The trend may be reversing.** By July 2026, software development postings had risen 15% since
February 2025 while overall postings fell 7% — and the more AI-exposed an occupation was, the more it
rebounded.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/08/ai-and-job-postings-from-destruction-to-creation/ -->

**Some numbers in circulation are simply wrong.** The most-cited figure for the junior-employment
decline was 13%, from the November 2025 version of the Stanford paper. After a methodological
revision, the same data vintage reads 15%, and the current headline is 19%. The 13%→15% change is a
methodology fix, not new data. If you see "13%" presented as current, the source is out of date.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/ -->

**And one source I could not read.** Yale's Budget Lab reportedly finds "no discernible disruption"
in the labour market — a direct challenge to this chapter's framing. Every attempt to open it failed;
I have only search snippets. I am telling you it exists rather than pretending it does not, and I am
not citing a number I could not read. Treat it as an open question.

**Finally:** none of this tells you what to do about your own situation, and a book cannot. It can
only tell you what the mirror shows, which is that the composition of valuable work changed and the
change favours judgment over procedure. What you do with that is genuinely yours.

## Do this today

1. **Under 30 minutes.** List the five tasks you did most often last week. Next to each, write
   "specifiable" or "not specifiable" — could a competent stranger produce it from a written
   description alone? Do not editorialize. Just sort.
2. **This week.** Take the one "not specifiable" task and write down where that knowledge came from —
   the specific episode that taught it. If you cannot name an episode, it may be codified after all,
   and you have learned something useful.
3. **This quarter.** Pick one judgment skill from your list and deliberately practise it *without* AI,
   on work where you would normally use it. Then compare the result to what the tool produces. The
   gap you notice is your actual current edge, measured rather than assumed.
