# 01. The Mirror

> **The one thing to take away:** there are two kinds of knowing — one you can look up, and one you
> have to earn — and AI just repriced them. The first got cheap. The second got scarce.

In early 2025, sixteen experienced open-source developers were asked a simple question about their
own work: how much faster would AI make them?

They knew these codebases. Some had maintained them for years. They guessed they would speed up by
about 24%.

The economists who designed the study guessed 39%.

The measured result was **19% slower**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089 -->

None of them was a beginner, and that is what makes the number worth sitting with. They were
not wrong because they did not know their craft. They were wrong because they mistook the *feeling*
of going faster for the fact of it. The tool answered instantly, confidently, in fluent code — and
fluent felt like fast.

Hold on to that mistake. It runs through this whole book.

## Why this matters now

Now zoom out from those sixteen developers to everyone who writes software for a living.

In February 2020, the market was theirs. Postings were abundant, entry-level roles existed, and the
path was legible: learn the language, build a portfolio, get hired, learn on the job, become senior.

By July 2025, software engineer postings were down **49%** from that February 2020 baseline. Android,
Java, .NET, iOS, web — the specific developer titles — were down more than 60%.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.hiringlab.org/2025/07/30/the-us-tech-hiring-freeze-continues/ -->

Here is the part almost nobody quotes. **Nearly half of that decline had already happened before
ChatGPT was released.**

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.hiringlab.org/2025/07/30/the-us-tech-hiring-freeze-continues/ -->

That single fact is why this book does not open with a triumphant story about AI.

If you are reading this because the ground moved under your feet, you are not imagining it. But you
are probably misdiagnosing why. The ground did move. AI is not the only thing that moved it. And the
skills you are afraid of losing may not be the ones that actually went.

The stakes are specific. Workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations are now employed
about **19% below** where they would be had they kept pace with their less-exposed peers. Experienced
workers show no comparable gap.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/ -->

A separate analysis of Census Bureau administrative data found early-career workers, aged 22 to 24,
in the most AI-exposed industries declining about **12%** over the ten quarters after ChatGPT's
release.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-27.html -->

Now notice what those numbers are *not* saying.

They are not saying the profession is dying. The Bureau of Labor Statistics still projects software
developers to grow **17.9%** between 2023 and 2033 — far faster than the 4.0% average across all
occupations.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2025/ai-impacts-in-bls-employment-projections.htm -->

The field is growing. The *door* is narrowing.

Those two facts are not in tension. Holding both at once is the first thing this book asks of you.

## Two kinds of knowing, and only one of them got cheap

The distinction that explains all of this is old. AI just gave it teeth.

Economists separate **codified knowledge** — the kind you could write into a textbook, a procedure, a
checklist — from **tacit knowledge**, the kind you get from doing the thing, failing, and adjusting.

Think of a recipe. *"Reduce the sauce by half"* is codified. Anyone can read it, anyone can follow
it. *"Take it off the heat when it stops looking like water and starts looking like a glaze"* is
tacit. Nobody can write down that exact moment. You learn it by ruining a few sauces.

The Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas put the consequence plainly. If a model can replicate codified
knowledge but not tacit knowledge, then AI will **automate** jobs that lean on textbook knowledge
while it **complements** jobs that demand experiential judgment.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0224 -->

Watch what that does to one labour market.

Employment for young workers in exposed occupations is falling, while wages in those same sectors
rise. Nationally, nominal average weekly wages rose 7.5% since fall 2022. In computer systems design,
they rose **16.7%**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0224 -->

Both at once — because *who gets hired* changed, not what the work is worth.

Picture what a new graduate actually brings to a first job. Syntax. Definitions. Standard patterns.
The textbook answer.

That is exactly the part that is now instant and free.

And here is the twist. For the graduate, those textbook tasks *are* the expert part of the job — the
thing they were hired to do. For someone with fifteen years of experience, the same tasks are the
*inexpert* part, the part they would happily hand off.

Dallas Fed makes the asymmetry explicit. A model can substitute for a worker who has book-learning but
no experience. It complements an experienced worker whose judgment cannot be replicated.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0224 -->

The job postings agree. Among software and tech listings, the share requiring **5+ years** of
experience rose from 37% to 42% between mid-2022 and mid-2025. The share asking for 2–4 years fell
from 46% to 40%.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.hiringlab.org/2025/07/30/experience-requirements-have-tightened-amid-the-tech-hiring-freeze/ -->

Now look at the occupations that were *not* part of the tech freeze. There, the same 5+ requirement
actually **fell**, from 16% to 11%.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.hiringlab.org/2025/07/30/experience-requirements-have-tightened-amid-the-tech-hiring-freeze/ -->

Read those two findings side by side, because the comparison is the whole chapter. Across the economy,
employers were loosening experience requirements. In the AI-exposed corner of it, they were tightening
them.

The market did not decide that experience is worthless. It decided that experience is the thing it
cannot get cheaply.

**Now the honest limit, and this book will keep doing this:** none of the research finds that *pay*
shifted toward judgment. Once you account for the experience premium, Dallas Fed finds no relationship
between a field's AI exposure and its post-2022 wage growth. The adjustment showed up in employment,
not in salaries.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0224 -->

If you were hoping this chapter would tell you that being wise now pays more, it will not.

### Why "it's just book learning" undersells the problem

It is tempting to hear "codified knowledge got cheap" and shrug. *I was never just memorising*, you
tell yourself. *I understand things.*

Maybe. But the test is not what you believe about your understanding. It is whether your output could
have been produced by someone who only read about it.

And the research says the gap between "understanding" and "assembling the plausible answer" is
narrower than we assume — even for experts.

In one study, people using an AI assistant wrote **significantly less secure code**. They were also
**more likely to believe they had written secure code**.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03622 -->

In another, 410 annotators given model suggestions did not get faster. They reported higher
confidence, and they "strongly took the LLM suggestions, significantly changing the label
distribution." The human in the loop rubber-stamped the output instead of checking it.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15821 -->

That last one should bother you. "A human reviews it" is the standard reassurance offered for AI
output. Often it is not a review at all. It is a signature.

## The mirror: what the market stopped paying for

So which of your own skills were closer to codified than you thought?

Not the ones you could defend in an argument. The ones your actual daily output depended on.

The data gives the pattern a name: **specifiable versus judgmental**. Watch it split one industry in
half.

Credit analysts are projected to decline 3.9%. Claims adjusters 4.4%. Insurance appraisers 9.2%.

Meanwhile software developers grow 17.9%, personal financial advisors 17.1%, database architects
10.8%.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2025/ai-impacts-in-bls-employment-projections.htm -->

And it is not "technical good, non-technical bad" — lawyers grow 5.2%, paralegals only 1.2%.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2025/ai-impacts-in-bls-employment-projections.htm -->

It is whether the work can be written down in advance.

A claims adjuster's job is largely rules applied to inputs. So is a paralegal's document review. A
financial advisor's job is mostly what happens when the rules run out and a frightened human being is
sitting across the desk. "Robo-advisors" have competed for years. Human advisors are still projected
to grow 17.1%.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2025/ai-impacts-in-bls-employment-projections.htm -->

That gives you a way to audit your own work that has nothing to do with your job title.

Take the tasks you did last week. For each one, ask: **could a competent stranger produce this from a
written description, without ever having done it?**

If yes, it is codified, and it is on the block. If no — if producing it needed something you only
know because you were there, or because you have watched this go wrong before — that is tacit. That
is where your value now sits.

Be honest about the ratio. Most of us find more codified work in our week than we expected. That
discovery is the point of the mirror. It is information, not a verdict.

### Why the mirror is more accurate than it used to be

Take this seriously now, rather than as a general observation about technology, because the failure is
no longer hypothetical — and no longer invisible to the client.

Legal research tools that vendors sold as "hallucination-free" hallucinate **17% to 33%** of the
time, according to the first preregistered evaluation of them. In the researchers' words, the vendors'
claims were overstated.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://reglab.stanford.edu/publications/hallucination-free-assessing-the-reliability-of-leading-ai-legal-research-tools/ -->

A survey of 319 knowledge workers found that the more confident someone was in generative AI, the
**less** critical thinking they applied. The thinking did not vanish. It shifted toward verification,
integration, and oversight of the tool.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/ -->

Notice what that implies. The premium is not disappearing. It is *moving* — from producing the first
draft to judging it.

And judging output you did not produce is harder than producing it, not easier. You have not been
through the process that would tell you where it went wrong.

That is the mirror's real message. Production got cheap. Telling good production from bad — when both
look fluent — got scarce. And you cannot get the second by reading about it, which is exactly why the
rest of this book is about practice.

## The test you can run on yourself

Everything so far is a claim about the labour market. This part is about you, and it is built to
produce an answer you will not enjoy.

Pick the thing you are best at — the skill you would name if someone asked what you bring.

Now ask three questions, in order.

**First: can it be specified?** Write down how you do it, in enough detail that a competent stranger
could follow it. Not a summary. The actual procedure. If you can write it down completely, you have
just shown it is codified — and you have sketched the thing you may soon be competing with.

**Second: where did it come from?** A course? A book? A tutorial? A standard practice? Or a specific
failure you still remember — the client who reacted badly, the system that broke at 2am, the estimate
that was wrong for a reason you only understood afterwards?

Codified knowledge can be learned in any order, by anyone, at any time. Tacit knowledge has a
biography. It arrived in a sequence, and the sequence is why you have it.

**Third — the one that actually separates them: would a model have known to ask?** Not "could a model
answer this." Could it have known the question mattered?

An experienced developer knows the obvious solution will not survive contact with the third use case.
She cannot always say why. That "cannot always say why" is the signal, not a weakness. If you can
fully articulate your judgment, it is closer to a rule than a judgment.

Most people land in an uncomfortable place: partly codified, partly not — with the codified part
being the part they were proudest of.

That is not a coincidence. Codified knowledge is *demonstrable*. Nobody compliments you for judgment
you cannot show. They compliment you for the deliverable.

Dallas Fed's experience-premium data gives this an economic shape. The median occupation has a 40% gap
between entry-level and experienced pay. For lawyers, underwriters, and credit analysts it rises above
100%. And the occupations with the highest AI exposure are, on average, the ones with the **highest**
experience premiums.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0224 -->

That is the mirror at its most precise. The market is not rewarding the field. It is pricing the
distance between someone who knows the procedure and someone who knows what the procedure is *for*.

### What the mirror does not say

It does not say to abandon your field, or to become a manager, or that technical skills are worthless.
The strongest growth projections in the BLS data are technical.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2025/ai-impacts-in-bls-employment-projections.htm -->

It does not say the professions are shrinking. Most are growing.

And it does not say experience is *safe*. The Stanford gap is between ages 22–25 and older workers. It
is silent about what happens to a 45-year-old whose own judgment has been quietly outsourced to a tool
for five years. That is Chapter 11.

What it says is narrow and usable: **the part of your skill that is a procedure is now cheap. The
part that is a decision is what you are paid for.**

Chapter 02 asks the next question — what, exactly, can machines not do? The four blind spots that make
the second half of that sentence structural rather than temporary. You need that answer before you can
decide what to build.

## The honest caveats

**The central evidence here is contested, and you should know how.**

The Stanford findings are described by their own authors as descriptive, not causal. The employment
gap shrinks when you control for education. Some of the divergent trends predate generative AI.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/ -->

The Census analysis attributes up to a quarter of the early-career decline to monetary-policy shocks.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-27.html -->

So "AI caused this" is not established. "This happened, and AI is a leading explanation" is.

**Strong counter-evidence exists, and I am not hiding it.**

A study of 25,000 Danish workers found effects on earnings and hours precisely estimated to be null —
ruling out anything larger than about 2% two years after ChatGPT. Employers absorbed AI mainly by
reorganising tasks, not by laying people off.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777 -->

Census data show only **2%** of firms reporting AI-related employment decreases, and 66% of AI users
using it purely to augment their work.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-25.html -->

**The strongest counter-example to this whole chapter** comes from customer support. AI raised
productivity 14% on average — but **34% for novice and low-skilled workers**, with minimal effect on
experienced ones. In that setting, AI *compressed* the experience premium instead of raising it.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161 -->

I take that seriously. It means the pattern here is not a law of nature. It depends on the work. Where
a job is mostly codified, AI helps novices catch up. Where judgment dominates, it does not. Support
work sits near one end of that scale — which is why the finding is real, and also why it does not
generalise for free.

**The trend may be reversing.** By July 2026, software development postings had risen 15% since
February 2025 while overall postings fell 7%. The more AI-exposed an occupation was, the more it
rebounded.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/08/ai-and-job-postings-from-destruction-to-creation/ -->

**Some numbers in circulation are simply wrong.** The most-cited figure for the junior-employment
decline was 13%, from the November 2025 version of the Stanford paper. After a methodological
revision, the same data vintage reads 15%. The current headline is 19%. That 13%→15% change is a
methodology fix, not new data. If you see "13%" presented as current, the source is out of date.

<!-- verified 2026-10-01 — source: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/ -->

**And one source I could not read.** Yale's Budget Lab reportedly finds "no discernible disruption" in
the labour market — a direct challenge to this chapter. Every attempt to open it failed; I have only
search snippets. I am telling you it exists rather than pretending it does not, and I am not citing a
number I could not read. Treat it as an open question.

**Finally:** none of this tells you what to do about your own situation. A book cannot. It can only
tell you what the mirror shows: the composition of valuable work changed, and the change favours
judgment over procedure. What you do with that is genuinely yours.

## Do this today

1. **Under 30 minutes.** List the five tasks you did most often last week. Next to each, write
   "specifiable" or "not specifiable" — could a competent stranger produce it from a written
   description alone? Do not editorialize. Just sort.
2. **This week.** Take the one "not specifiable" task and write down where that knowledge came from —
   the specific episode that taught it. If you cannot name an episode, it may be codified after all,
   and you have learned something useful.
3. **This quarter.** Pick one judgment skill from your list and deliberately practise it *without* AI,
   on work where you would normally use it. Then compare the result to what the tool produces. The gap
   you notice is your actual current edge, measured rather than assumed.
